| **0:00–0:30** Problem & headline | Title slide → one results table (validation D², private score) | Hello, I'm Suguru Fujita. The task was to predict the expected 2025 claim cost for each policy across motor, home buildings and home contents, scored by Tweedie D². I built an AI-assisted, human-governed pricing workflow. In Week 1 the final model reached a private D² of 0.134, close to our own temporal-validation estimate of 0.139, and finished 30th. In Week 2 we moved the model to an emerging market with only two years of history, and priced the new business that dominates its 2025 book. |
| **0:30–1:00** Target first | Reconciliation table (12 cells, diff 0.00) | There was no target column, so the first job was to rebuild it from the claims data: incurred minus recoveries, declined claims excluded, each claim matched to the policy row whose period contains the loss date. Claim level and row level reconcile to the cent in every line and year. |
| **1:00–1:45** Actuarial approach | Development factors chart; trend low/central/high table; model ladder table | Claims were valued at the end of 2024, so recent years are immature. We used cost-based reporting-delay factors, and for bodily injury, where case estimates looked short, an expected-severity approach. Trends come from mix-adjusted GLM year effects; for example, motor's falling claim frequency turned out to be mostly portfolio mix. The model is a 50/50 blend of a Tweedie GLM, which anchors explainability, and a regularised LightGBM, with large-loss caps and a loading. On temporal validation the blend improves pooled D² from 0.082 for a constant to 0.139. |
| **1:45–2:15** AI workflow | Workflow diagram: AI builds & challenges → tests/validation → human gates G1–G5 → logs | Claude Code wrote most of the code and analysis, but it worked inside rules I set: act as a challenger, stop at six human review gates, and log every contribution. The repository holds 37 signed-off decisions and an AI usage log. |
| **2:15–3:00** Three real examples | Split screen: before/after for each case | Three examples. First, the AI inferred that claim amounts were gross of excess and subtracted it again; the official data description said otherwise, and we corrected it before any modelling. Second, the first gradient-boosting model was not level-balanced; on validation, actual costs came out up to 26 percent above its predictions, and a balance step fixed it. Third, the AI proposed recency weighting; it looked good on 2024 alone, but our rule requires a gain in both validation years, and it failed 2023, so we did not adopt it. |
| **3:00–4:00** Week 2: what changed and how we adapted | New-business share 14% → 76%; A/E table (motor 0.71, buildings 0.89, contents 0.86); carried-over vs changed table; NB probe table; uncertainty low/central/high | In the emerging market, the biggest change is the customer mix: 76 percent of the 2025 test book is new business, against 14 percent in training. Our Week 1 model, applied to emerging history, predicted too high: actual costs came out 11 to 29 percent below its predictions, so the level does not transfer. The shape mostly does: most rating factors behave alike in both markets. So we kept the developed model's shape and re-fitted the level on emerging data; for buildings, a joint fit across both markets validated clearly better. The hardest judgement was new business, which training data barely covers. Leaderboard probes, registered in advance, supported a loading of 1.25, and a uniform-level probe suggested the effect is specific to new business. Much of that evidence comes from the leaderboard, so we report it as uncertain: a range of 1.0 to 1.5, and the possibility that the emerging level itself is low. |
| **4:00–4:30** Guardrails | Test run (64 passed); decision log; config file | Every transform has a unit test, including leakage checks. Acceptance rules were written before results were seen, and leaderboard probes were registered with their expected outcome. Rebuilding the submissions on a later day reproduced them byte for byte. |
| **4:30–5:00** Pricing tool, office premium & close | Pricing tool demo; office premium worked example (B00229821) | Finally, the pricing tool: enter a policy and it returns the expected cost with a factor-by-factor breakdown from the GLM, plus the model and assumption versions used. For the 20 emerging reference policies we built the office premium from our risk premium. It comes out at a median 2.7 times today's premium, mainly because broker commission and fixed expenses are heavy in this market. That is a question about the profitability of the broker book. Thank you. |
